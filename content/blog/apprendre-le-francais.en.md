Say I catch *la pluie* in a French weather forecast. Okay, rain. But did it rain this morning, or is it going to rain tomorrow? If I can tell the difference, I’ve understood something I could actually use. I like starting French practice with a question that small.

## Guess before turning on the sound

[TV5MONDE](https://apprendre.tv5monde.com/fr) has a free A1 course called **Première classe**. Try watching a scene on mute. Do the two people know each other, or are they meeting for the first time? Now there’s something I want to find out when I turn the sound back on.

## One story, one sentence in my notebook

My favourite thing about [RFI’s Journal en français facile](https://francaisfacile.rfi.fr/fr/podcasts/journal-en-fran%C3%A7ais-facile/) is that I can read what I just heard. Even with *facile* in the name, I don’t expect to follow a whole broadcast. If I have fifteen minutes, I’d pick one story.

I’d listen, write one sentence from memory, then open the transcript to check the bit I wasn’t sure about. If the presenter said *il va pleuvoir*, for instance, the rain hasn’t happened yet. I’d miss that little detail if all I wrote down was *pluie = rain*.

## What gave the answer away?

[France Éducation international](https://www.france-education-international.fr/diplome/delf-junior-scolaire) has free DELF junior/scolaire sample papers by level. For a reading question, imagine explaining your answer to someone in your class. Which word would you point to? That’s a pretty good way to tell whether you understood the sentence or just guessed the right box.

I wouldn’t try to finish all three sites in one evening. For today, I’d settle for finding out whether my French is good enough to tell me if I need an umbrella tomorrow.
